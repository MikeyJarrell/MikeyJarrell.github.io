Pb Action: Cost-Benefit Analysis

Country prioritization

Countries ranked by the probability of success the Battery Index needs

The countries where the Battery Index clears Coefficient Giving’s bar with the smallest chance of success come first. Sorting by the harm from smelting puts first the countries where the most harm is done. Every figure is at our default inputs; click a country to open its analysis and change them.

Probability of success

The probability that the published index leads the country’s batteries to move from the unsafe recyclers to good ones. Nothing here estimates it. When it is the sufficient statistic, the answer is how large it has to be; otherwise it is an input, and 50 percent is a placeholder. The minimum sufficient reallocation share is the share of the batteries now at unsafe recyclers that has to move to good ones for the index to be a good investment, with success taken as certain. The two enter the equation the same way, so they are the same number.

Unsafe share

Batteries go to three kinds of recycler: good industrial plants, bad industrial plants and informal smelters. Within a kind, the lead released per ton is taken to be roughly the same everywhere. The unsafe share is the bad industrial and informal shares together. If the index works, this is the share that moves.

The shares are of batteries, not of sites: informal smelters are far smaller than plants, so a count of sites overstates them. Informal means a pit or a backyard furnace; an industrial plant that never registered counts as industrial.

Every other country’s shares are our estimate, in three steps.

The informal share, where there is an industrial smelter. 30 percent in South and East Asia, 5 percent everywhere else. India’s 30 percent is the one figure with a source that counts batteries at informal smelters; in Nigeria and Ghana, industrial plants have driven informal smelters out, because a furnace recovers 90 percent or more of the lead and a backyard melt 50 to 60, so plants can pay more for a battery (Manhart and Adjei, 2026). In South Asia informal smelters also sell raw lead to industrial plants for refining; a battery is counted where it is smelted first.

The good share of the industrial tons. It rises with income: none at $4,000 of GDP per person, all at $45,000, and between them the square of the distance along a logarithmic income scale. That gives 14 percent at India’s income, 9 at Nigeria’s, 44 at Brazil’s and 81 at Malaysia’s. Two points fix the curve, India (10 of 70) and Nigeria (one plant in nine). Nothing checks it at higher incomes.

Countries with no industrial smelter on record. The records miss plants: they list none in Bangladesh, the Philippines or Tanzania, which have them. So a country with none on record is averaged over two cases. If it truly has none, everything it smelts is informal. If it has one the records missed, the two steps above apply. Safety gap

How many times the harm per ton of a good industrial plant the country’s unsafe recyclers do, on average over their batteries:

Safety gap=246×Bad share+3,531×Informal shareBad share+Informal share

Bad industrial plants are counted at 246 times a good plant. That is the three Ghana plants in the Battery Index pilot, which release 1.9 kilograms of airborne lead per ton of batteries, against the 7.6 grams of a good plant (see Harm averted).

Informal smelters are counted at 3,531 times: 27 kilograms of airborne lead per ton at an uncontrolled smelter. This is a model value. Nothing has measured it, and the plausible range runs from a seventeenth of it to nineteen times it.

The average is over the unsafe batteries only, because the equation multiplies the safety gap by the unsafe share. The shares are those under Unsafe share.

Scale

Tons of used batteries smelted in the country per year. The Battery Index acts on smelting, so the scale is the smelting that happens in the country. Batteries exported as scrap count toward the country that smelts them, and a country that exports all of its used batteries has a scale of zero.

Ben Savonen’s lead economy map (2026) gives two figures for every country, in tons of lead, from recorded trade for 2022 to 2024 and reported production. One is the lead available to the country’s smelters: the lead in batteries reaching the end of their life, 95 percent of it collected, plus scrap batteries imported, less scrap batteries exported. The other is the lead its smelters must have taken in to produce what they report. The scale is the larger of the two, divided by 0.65, the share of a battery’s weight that is lead.

It is a floor, since it misses unrecorded flows. For Nigeria and Ghana the literature has better figures (Manhart and Adjei 2025; SRI Policy Brief 2024).

Côte d’Ivoire exported all of its used batteries in the trade record. It suspended those exports in March 2025 and has a new smelter, so its scale is everything it collects.

The map at the top of the main page shows a different figure: batteries reaching the end of their life in the country, wherever they are smelted.

Harm averted

Per ton moved: the harm per ton at the unsafe recyclers less the harm per ton at a high-standard plant, hgood, in cpBLL-years per ton; the unsafe recyclers’ harm is the safety gap times hgood. A high-standard plant here is a plant like Green Recycling in Nigeria, taken to meet Europe’s legal limit for lead in stack gas (1 milligram per cubic meter of stack gas, 8 to 24 grams of lead per ton of refined lead at a secondary smelter’s gas flows): 7.6 grams of airborne lead for each ton of batteries fed, stack only. Europe’s best plants achieve a third of that (2.4 grams, pooled from nine measured plants and national factors); India’s 1998 standard permits ten times more. Nothing measures Green Recycling’s stack; this is an assumption. hgood follows from the three Ghana plants, whose harm per ton, 5.54 cpBLL-years, is scaled down by the ratio of that figure to the Ghana plants’ 1.9 kilograms, since at a given place harm scales with what is released. The Ghana figure counts a ton’s whole future: lead deposited on the ground leaves the top two centimeters at 3.8 percent per year, and every year it is there it raises the blood lead of the people living on it, by distance, through two fitted curves, one for dust released at ground level and one for what leaves the stack (Battery Index pilot, Ghana, 2026). It is the mean over the fit’s uncertainty, not the median. Nothing is discounted.

Benefit

The value of one cpBLL-year: one person’s blood lead one µg/dL higher for one year. Children’s future earnings for the under-five share of the population, from Coefficient Giving’s lead impact calculator (0.145 IQ points per µg/dL, 0.67 percent of wages per point, 35 working years: CG$ 840 billion for lowering one year’s births by 4.29 µg/dL, spread over a five-year window of childhood), plus everyone’s health: one year of the world’s lead DALYs (23.5 million, GBD 2021 cut by a quarter, as the calculator counts them) at CG$ 100,000 each, over the world’s blood lead (population × 4.1 µg/dL, Larsen and Sánchez-Triana 2023). In US dollars, the cell shows what the funder would pay to get it at its bar (CG$ ÷ 1,000); in DALYs, CG$ ÷ 100,000.

Acceleration

How many years earlier the country ends dirty recycling than it would have on its own. The expected probability of solving the issue in any given year depends on income, and increases over time as technology advances and enforcement becomes easier; the cell shows the expected years until a yearly weighted coin comes up. The weight is 1 ÷ (1 + e−(a + b × ln income + c × (year − 2000))), with income growing at the country’s twenty-year average (World Bank). The idea is Dasgupta et al. (2002): pollution falls with income, and the income at which it falls comes down over time as technology improves and enforcement gets easier. The calendar term c comes from how fast leaded gasoline was phased out across 163 countries (UNEP Partnership for Clean Fuels and Vehicles; Our World in Data); a and b from which countries have ended dirty battery recycling and when (36 have, 102 low- and middle-income countries have not by 2025), over 6,879 country-years of income. Doubling income multiplies the yearly odds by 609; each decade multiplies them by 9.3.

Cost

What Pb Action spends to deploy the Battery Index in the country, in US dollars:

Cost=$50,000+$24,000×Industrial smelters0.75

The fixed cost covers the implementation partner, travel, equipment and government consulting fees. The second part is the cost of assessing the smelters. Each further smelter costs less than the one before: the largest are assessed first, and far down the list a small plant may not need an assessment of its own. The curve for this second part passes through what assessing the smelters cost in the two pilots, $55,000 for 3 smelters in Ghana and $115,000 for 8 in Nigeria. The pilots’ lead isotope work ($20,000 in Ghana, $70,000 in Nigeria) is left out, since future countries will not have it. All of these are rough figures from Pb Action’s budget, recalled from memory, and two countries cannot show how the cost behaves at hundreds of smelters.

With the fixed cost added, that is about $105,000 for Ghana, $164,000 for Nigeria, $74,000 for a country with one smelter and $3.2 million for India’s 675. Pb Action has planned on about $150,000 per country to be conservative, which the equation gives at about seven smelters; the February 2026 proposal to Coefficient Giving put a country assessment at $180,000 at most.

The number of industrial smelters is the count in Ben Savonen’s lead economy map (2026), the registered plants in Tanzania (7) and Bangladesh (6), and one where none is on record. The count for India is its 675 registered recyclers.

Burden

The share of the country’s lead exposure that battery recycling causes, which the inputs above imply: the recycling’s yearly exposure (scale × harm per ton, counting unsafe and high-standard tons at their own rates; a steady stream of tons produces each year the whole-future harm of one year’s tons) over the country’s yearly exposure from all sources (population × mean blood lead for its income group, from GBD 2019 by way of Larsen and Sánchez-Triana 2023: 6.6 µg/dL in low-income countries, 5.4 in lower-middle, 3.3 in upper-middle). Crawfurd et al. (2026) estimate it from a census of polluted sites and a 5 km exposure radius, at 36 percent across low- and middle-income countries and 35.6 in sub-Saharan Africa. Setting the burden to their figure sets the safety gap to what would produce it.

How the calculation works

The Battery Index is worth funding in a country where its expected return on cost clears Coefficient Giving’s bar:

Return=p×Unsafe share×Scale×Harm averted×Benefit×AccelerationCost≥1,000

where p is the probability that the index achieves its goals, and

Harm averted=hgood×(Safety gap−1)Burden=Scale×(Unsafe share×Safety gap+1−Unsafe share)×hgoodPopulation×Blood lead

This is one equation with one unknown. The sufficient statistic picks which term is solved for: the minimum sufficient probability of success is the p at which the return is exactly 1,000 with every other term at its input; the maximum cost is the cost at which it is, and so on.
